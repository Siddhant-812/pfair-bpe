\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Experimental setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\noindent\textbf{Languages and data.}
We use Hindi, Marathi and Gujarati, for which gold morphological segmentations are
available \citep{rathore2026}, together with English as the dominant language. Training text
comes from the verified split of Sangraha \citep{khan2024}, with about 20 million characters
for each Indian language and 80 million for English, so that English has four times the text
of each other language. We NFC-normalise all lines and remove lines shorter than 20
characters and exact duplicates. Parallel text comes from FLORES-200 \citep{nllb2022}: we use
the \emph{dev} split (997 sentences) during training to measure the costs $t_\ell$, and the
\emph{devtest} split (1012 sentences) only for the reported numbers.

\noindent\textbf{Tokenizers.}
All tokenizers are character-level BPE with whitespace pre-tokenization, a word-start marker
and a vocabulary of 32{,}000. The base alphabet contains every character that occurs at least
five times in the training text (about 1000 characters), and rarer characters map to an
unknown token. We train $p\in\{1,2,4,8,16,32,\infty\}$, where $p=1$ is classical BPE and
$p=\infty$ is our implementation of the base rule of Parity-aware BPE. As the main baseline,
we use the \textbf{hybrid} schedule of \citet{foroutan2026}, which learns the first 50\%,
75\% or 90\% of the merges with the classical rule and the rest with the parity rule. Both
baselines are our own implementations of the published rules; the original is byte-level,
and we do not implement its moving-window variant.

\noindent\textbf{Protocol.}
To measure variance due to the training data, we train every configuration on five
non-overlapping samples of Sangraha and report the mean and standard deviation. To separate
this from variance due to the test set, we additionally resample the devtest sentences 1000
times with replacement (a paired bootstrap). Metrics are defined in
Section~\ref{sec:problem}.

\noindent\textbf{Implementation and checks.}
We implement the trainer in Rust with no external dependencies, and verify it in three ways.
First, the lazy trainer produces merge lists identical to full rescoring for all values of
$p$ and for the hybrid on a small corpus, and identical 32k vocabularies for $p=4$ and
$p=8$ on the full corpus. Second, the $p=1$ setting reproduces, merge for merge, an
independent textbook implementation of BPE. Third, the evaluator's token counts on the
training text equal the trainer's own totals. On a two-core machine, a 32k tokenizer trains
in about 15 seconds on 140 million characters, against about 5 minutes with full rescoring,
and the lazy heap re-checks on average 3 entries per merge at $p=1$ and 30 at $p=8$.

\subsection{Classical BPE is unfair when one language dominates}

Table~\ref{tab:perlang} reports the number of tokens each tokenizer needs for the same
sentence. Under classical BPE, a Hindi sentence costs 35.0 tokens and its English
translation 29.0, a difference of 21\%. The cause is visible in the last column of
Table~\ref{tab:front}: English, with four sevenths of the training text, leads 49\% of the
merges.

\subsection{Raising $p$ traces the compression--parity front}

As $p$ grows, Hindi and Gujarati become cheaper, English becomes more expensive, and at
$p=\infty$ the four costs meet near 32 tokens (Table~\ref{tab:perlang};
Fig.~\ref{fig:perlang} in the appendix). Table~\ref{tab:front} and Fig.~\ref{fig:front}
give the two summary measures. The Gini coefficient falls at every step, from 0.038 to
0.003, while compression of the training text falls at every step, from 6.91 to 6.63 bytes
per token.

The price of parity is uneven along the front. At $p=8$, the Gini coefficient is 40\% lower
than under classical BPE, while compression is 0.6\% lower. At $p=16$, the reduction is 60\%
for 1.3\%, and full parity costs 4.0\%. Thus, a large part of the disparity can be removed
for a small part of the cost of removing all of it.

\subsection{The front is reliable}

Every increase in $p$ lowers both the Gini coefficient and the compression of the training
text in five of five samples. The standard deviation of the Gini coefficient across samples
is between 0.0001 and 0.0005, which is 10 to 30 times smaller than the difference between
neighbouring values of $p$. Resampling the test sentences leads to the same conclusion: the
95\% interval for classical BPE is $[0.036,0.040]$ and for $p=8$ it is $[0.021,0.025]$, and
each step up in $p$ lowers the Gini coefficient in at least 99.7\% of the 1000 resamples.

\subsection{Comparison with the hybrid baseline}

The hybrid points in Fig.~\ref{fig:front} lie on the $p$-fair front. To compare the two
methods at equal compression, we interpolate the $p$-fair front at the compression of each
hybrid tokenizer, separately in each sample, and take the difference in Gini
(Table~\ref{tab:hybrid}). The differences are about 0.001 and consistent in sign across the
five samples, but go in both directions: $p$-fair BPE is slightly fairer at 75\%, whereas the
hybrid is slightly fairer at 50\% and 90\%. Thus, neither method dominates the other on the
front. Moreover, the hybrid with 50\% classical merges is at least as good as pure
Parity-aware BPE on both measures (0.0023 vs.\ 0.0028 in Gini, 6.65 vs.\ 6.63 in
compression), in line with the motivation for the hybrid in \citet{foroutan2026}.

For every value of $p$ we test, no Indian language costs more than under classical BPE.
In contrast, the parity phase of the hybrid equalises the three Indian languages with one
another, which raises Marathi above its classical cost at 90\% and 75\% classical merges,
by up to 0.8 tokens per sentence (33.26 vs.\ 32.47 at 75\%). At similar compression, $p$-fair BPE
with $p=8$ keeps Marathi at 31.90 while leaving a wider spread between the Indian languages.
Consequently, the choice between the two depends on the goal: the hybrid moves closer to
equal cost among the Indian languages, whereas $p$-fair BPE makes none of them worse off
than classical BPE.

\subsection{Compression on balanced test text}

On devtest, where every language has the same content, intermediate values of $p$ compress
better than classical BPE (8.89 vs.\ 8.72 bytes per token at $p=16$; Table~\ref{tab:front}).
Classical BPE optimises compression of the training mixture, which is mostly English,
whereas a balanced test set rewards the vocabulary that $p$-fair BPE moves to the other
three languages. Thus, the loss in Fig.~\ref{fig:front} is a loss in compression of the
training distribution, and whether it is a real cost depends on the mixture of languages the
tokenizer meets in deployment.
