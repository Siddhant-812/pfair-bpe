\section{Ablations and Analysis}
\label{sec:ablation}

\subsection{The cost unit decides the outcome}
\label{sec:unitresult}

To test the choice of cost unit (Section~\ref{sec:problem}), we trained the parity rule
with the cost measured per byte and per character instead of per parallel sentence
(Table~\ref{tab:byte}, Appendix~\ref{app:unit}). Per byte, English becomes the worst-off language, the parity rule gives it 99\% of the
merges (89\% at $p=8$), and the Indian languages need about twice as many tokens per
sentence as under classical BPE (63.6 vs.\ 35.0 for Hindi) while English needs fewer (26.6
vs.\ 29.0). Per character the inversion disappears, but sentence length in characters
differs by language (a Gujarati devtest sentence has 4\% fewer characters than its Hindi
translation), so the per-character parity tokenizer leaves a Gini of 0.010 per sentence,
against 0.003 for the per-sentence rule, with English the most expensive language. The cost
should therefore be measured on parallel text.


\subsection{Data balance and vocabulary size}
\label{sec:sens}

We repeated the experiment with other amounts of English and larger vocabularies
(Table~\ref{tab:sens}, Appendix~\ref{app:sens}; single runs unless stated). Gini and
compression fall at every step in $p$ in every setting, but the size of the problem depends
strongly on data balance: with equal amounts of text classical BPE is already close to
parity (Gini 0.019, with English between Marathi and Hindi) and full parity costs 1.1\% of
compression, whereas with eight times as much English the Gini coefficient is 0.051, a
Hindi sentence costs 30\% more tokens than its English translation, and full parity costs
7.0\%.

Vocabulary size changes the picture in a way we did not expect. Between 32k and 256k the
Gini coefficient of classical BPE (0.038 at every size) and of Parity-aware BPE (0.003 to
0.004) do not move, so the loss of equity that \citet{lee2026} report for Parity-aware BPE
at larger vocabularies does not appear here, whereas the Gini coefficient at intermediate
$p$ rises, from 0.023 to 0.032 at $p=8$ and from 0.008 to 0.020 at $p=32$, and the cost of
full parity grows from 4.0\% to 10.7\%. English leads 39\% of the merges at $p=8$ at every size, so the dominant language does not
acquire more merges; rather, the spread among the Indian languages grows (Hindi 27.9 vs.\
Marathi 24.1 tokens per sentence at 256k and $p=8$), and $p=32$ raises Marathi above its
classical cost from 64k on (by up to 4.8\% at 256k). The exponent needed for a given level
of parity thus grows with the vocabulary. The comparison with the hybrid moves the other way. At 128k, in five samples of training
text, $p$-fair BPE is fairer than all three hybrid points at matched compression, by
$0.0023\pm0.0003$ (50\% classical merges), $0.0059\pm0.0002$ (75\%) and $0.0022\pm0.0001$
(90\%) in Gini, in five of five samples each, whereas at 32k the differences are about 0.001
with both signs (Section~\ref{sec:hybrid}); single runs at 64k and 256k show the same sign
(0.0003 to 0.0075). Thus, the schedules separate where the vocabulary is large.


\subsection{Vocabulary allocation across scripts}
\label{sec:alloc}

How should a fixed vocabulary of size $K$ be divided between scripts? We fitted a cost curve
$t_c(k)=a_c k^{-b_c}+d_c$ per script $c$ from tokenizers trained on its languages alone at
five budgets from 2k to 32k (root-mean-square error at most 0.03 tokens per sentence for
three scripts and 0.15 for ten) and solved $\min_k \sum_c \pi_c\, t_c(k_c)^p$ subject to
$\sum_c k_c=K$, which is convex for $p\ge1$, by water-filling (Appendix~\ref{app:alloc}).
The merged tokenizers were compared with a uniform split, a data-proportional split, the
union-proportion rule of \citet{brahma2026} and a joint tokenizer of the same size
(Table~\ref{tab:alloc}; budgets in Appendix~\ref{app:alloc}).

The convex split does what the objective asks of it, and splitting by script costs little.
At $p=1$ it is the best-compressing split in both settings, within 0.05\% of the joint
tokenizer with three scripts (6.901 vs.\ 6.904 bytes per token) and 0.14\% with ten,
whereas the uniform split loses 1.7\% and 4.3\%. At $p=8$ it moves budget towards the scripts
whose languages are expensive and lowers the Gini coefficient of the split from 0.038 to
0.026 with three scripts and from 0.065 to 0.052 with ten, at the compression of the joint
$p=8$ tokenizer; allocation is nevertheless a weaker lever than the merge rule, since the
joint $p=8$ tokenizer reaches 0.023 and 0.037 at that compression.
The union-proportion split is fairer (0.019 and 0.048) but pays 2.7\% and 0.8\% of
compression for it, and the data-proportional split is the least fair in both settings
(0.051 and 0.084). Finally, allocation acts on clusters rather than languages: with ten scripts, the
convex $p=8$ split raises Assamese, the smallest language, from 42.4 to 45.5 tokens per
sentence, because its cluster's cost is dominated by Bengali, which has 26 times as much
text. A fair split between scripts does not remove the need for a fair rule within them.

\subsection{Scaling to 14 languages and natural data balance}
\label{sec:multi}

With one dominant language a single switch point suffices to trace the front, which may
explain why the hybrid matches $p$-fair BPE. To test the rule where several languages are
under-served at different levels, we trained on 14 languages in 10 scripts (Hindi, Marathi,
Gujarati, Bengali, Punjabi, Odia, Assamese, Sanskrit, Tamil, Telugu, Kannada, Malayalam,
Urdu and English) with text in proportion to each Sangraha split, so that Hindi (75M
characters) has 33 times the text of Assamese (2.2M), at 64k with $p$ up to 128, on three
samples (Table~\ref{tab:multi}; per-language costs in Appendix~\ref{app:multi}).

The disparity is larger and no longer an English problem: under classical BPE the Gini
coefficient is 0.067$\pm$0.001, a Punjabi sentence costs 43.6 tokens against 30.4 for
Bengali, and English, with 15\% of the text, is the second-cheapest language and leads only
14\% of the merges. Raising $p$ again traces a monotone front in three of three samples,
from 0.067 to 0.004 in Gini and from 8.94 to 8.34 bytes per token; at $p=8$ the Gini
coefficient falls by 44\% for 1.0\% of compression and at $p=16$ by 62\% for 2.1\%.

At matched compression, the outcome depends on where the comparison is made. At the fair
end, $p$-fair BPE is fairer than the hybrid with 50\% classical merges by
$+0.0030\pm0.0003$ in Gini (3 of 3 samples); at 75\% the two tie ($-0.0002\pm0.0001$); at 90\% the
hybrid is fairer by $-0.0023\pm0.0002$ (3 of 3). Worst-$N$ with $N\in\{2,3,5\}$ and the moving
window again land at the parity end, within 0.0002 of the front. These differences are ten times the sample-to-sample spread (at most 0.0007) but about 5\%
of the span of the front, so the schedules still trace the same curve as $p$-fair BPE: the
front is determined by the data rather than by the rule, and neither interpolation dominates.

The rules differ in who pays. Every rule that moves towards parity, $p$-fair BPE included,
raises the cheapest languages: Bengali goes from 30.4 to 32.7 tokens per sentence at $p=8$
and to 37.3 at $p=\infty$, and Hindi from 33.0 to 33.8 and 36.9 (Appendix~\ref{app:multi}),
so the four-language observation that no Indian language becomes more expensive does not
generalise. What does hold concerns the under-served languages, those above the classical
mean cost (Punjabi, Odia, Assamese, Sanskrit, Kannada, Malayalam and Urdu, plus Gujarati in
two of three samples): $p$-fair BPE never raises any of them at any $p$ (largest cost ratio
against classical BPE 0.968 to 0.995), whereas the hybrid raises one to three of them in
every sample (Malayalam in all three, Gujarati in two, Kannada in one), by up to 1.2\% at
75\% and 2.3\% at 90\% classical merges (last column of Table~\ref{tab:multi}). The parity
rule, Worst-$N$ and the window also spare the under-served languages, but only at the parity
end of the front.

\subsection{Language-model evaluation (planned)}
\label{sec:downstream}

Token cost is what a user pays, but a fairer tokenizer could in principle make the model
worse at the languages it helps. To test this, we train one small language model per
tokenizer on the same training text (Appendix~\ref{app:impl}) and report bits per byte on
FLORES devtest, a measure that does not depend on the tokenization
(table to be added). Across tokenizers, the mean bits per byte differs by at most
--, and the spread between languages changes from -- under classical BPE to -- at $p=8$.
Optionally, we also report the number of tokens per technical term on CSTT term lists.
