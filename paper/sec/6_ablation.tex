\subsection{The cost unit decides the outcome}
\label{sec:unitresult}

To test the choice of cost unit (Section~\ref{sec:problem}), we trained the parity rule
with the cost measured per byte and per character instead of per parallel sentence
(Table~\ref{tab:byte}). Per byte, English becomes the worst-off language, and the parity
rule gives it 99\% of the merges. Measured by what a user pays, the result is the opposite
of the intention: the Indian languages need about twice as many tokens per sentence as
under classical BPE (63.6 vs.\ 35.0 for Hindi), while English needs fewer (26.6 vs.\ 29.0).
The inversion is not confined to the parity end, since at $p=8$ the per-byte cost still
sends 89\% of the merges to English and raises Hindi to 45.4 tokens per sentence. Per
character, the inversion disappears because every script spends one code point per
character, but the rule then equalises tokens per character rather than per sentence, and
sentence length in characters differs by language (a Gujarati devtest sentence has 4\%
fewer characters than its Hindi translation). The per-character parity tokenizer therefore
leaves a Gini coefficient of 0.010 per sentence, against 0.003 for the per-sentence rule,
and makes English the most expensive language (32.9 vs.\ 31.3 tokens for Gujarati). Thus, a
per-byte cost inverts the parity rule across scripts, a per-character cost only
approximates shared content, and the cost should be measured on parallel text.

\subsection{Data balance and vocabulary size}
\label{sec:sens}

To measure how much the disparity depends on the setting, we repeated the experiment with
other amounts of English and with larger vocabularies (Table~\ref{tab:sens}). These are
single runs, whereas the five-sample spread of the main setting is at most 0.0005 in Gini.
The direction is the same in every setting: the Gini coefficient and the compression fall
at every step in $p$. However, the size of the problem changes considerably with data
balance. With equal amounts of text, classical BPE is already close to parity (Gini
0.019), and full parity costs 1.1\% of compression. With eight times as much English,
the Gini coefficient is 0.051, a Hindi sentence costs 30\% more tokens than its English
translation, and full parity costs 7.0\%. Moreover, with equal data the remaining
disparity is not between English and the Indian languages, since English (31.7 tokens)
lies between Marathi (30.4) and Hindi (33.3).

Vocabulary size changes the picture in a way we did not expect. Between 32k and 256k, the
Gini coefficient of classical BPE (0.038 at every size) and of Parity-aware BPE
(0.003 to 0.004) do not move, so the loss of equity that \citet{lee2026} report for
Parity-aware BPE at larger vocabularies does not appear here, possibly because the share of
English is held constant and the number of languages is small. In contrast, the Gini
coefficient at intermediate $p$ rises with the vocabulary, from 0.023 to 0.032 at
$p=8$ and from 0.008 to 0.020 at $p=32$, and the cost of full parity grows from
4.0\% to 10.7\%. We had expected the opposite, since every language keeps a positive
weight in Eq.~\eqref{eq:rule} while the parity rule bounds only the worst-off language. The
share of merges led by English at $p=8$ is 39\% at every size, so the dominant language
does not acquire more merges; rather, the spread among the Indian languages grows, with
Hindi at 27.9 tokens per sentence against 24.1 for Marathi at 256k and $p=8$, and
$p=32$ raises Marathi above its classical cost at 64k and above (by 0.6\% at 64k and
4.8\% at 256k). Thus, the exponent needed for a given level of parity grows with the
vocabulary. The comparison with the hybrid moves the other way: at 64k, 128k and 256k,
$p$-fair BPE is fairer than all three hybrid points at matched compression, by
0.0003 to 0.0075 in Gini in single runs, whereas at 32k the differences have both signs
(Section~\ref{sec:hybrid}).

\subsection{Morphology penalty (planned)}
\label{sec:morph}

To add morphological alignment as a third axis, we subtract a penalty for merges that join
symbols from different morphemes:
\begin{equation}
  (a,b)^\star = \argmax_{(a,b)}\; s_p(a,b) - \lambda\,\beta(a,b),
  \label{eq:morph}
\end{equation}
where $\beta(a,b)=\sum_\ell t_\ell^{\,p-1}\beta_\ell(a,b)$ and $\beta_\ell(a,b)$ counts the
occurrences of the pair in language $\ell$ whose junction is a morpheme boundary according
to the SuTRA segmentations \citep{rathore2026}; an occurrence that crosses a boundary thus
contributes $(1-\lambda)$ of its weight instead of 1. For $0\le\lambda\le1$ every term of
the score stays non-negative and non-increasing, so Proposition~\ref{prop:lazy} and the
lazy trainer remain exact; $\lambda=1$ ignores crossing occurrences entirely. Boundaries are
known only for words in the segmentation lexicon, and the lexicon is disjoint from the
held-out words on which boundary F1 is measured. Table~\ref{tab:morph} reports boundary F1
against the held-out SuTRA gold segmentations. Increasing $\lambda$ changes boundary F1 by
--, at a cost of -- in Gini and -- in compression.

\subsection{Vocabulary allocation across scripts (planned)}
\label{sec:alloc}

A complementary question is how a fixed vocabulary of size $K$ should be divided between
scripts (Devanagari, Gujarati and Latin). We fit a cost curve $t_c(k)=a_c k^{-b_c}+d_c$ per
script $c$ and solve $\min_k \sum_c \pi_c\, t_c(k_c)^p$ subject to $\sum_c k_c=K$, which is
convex for $p\ge1$ and is solved by water-filling (Appendix~\ref{app:alloc}). We compare it
with a uniform split, a split proportional to the data share of each script, and the
union-proportion rule of \citet{brahma2026}, and we report a joint tokenizer of the same
size without any allocation as a reference (Table~\ref{tab:alloc}).

\subsection{Scaling to 14 languages and natural data balance (planned)}
\label{sec:multi}

With four languages and one dominant language, the front is one-dimensional and a single
switch point suffices to trace it, which may explain why the hybrid schedule matches
$p$-fair BPE. To test the rule where several languages are under-served at different
levels, we train on 14 languages in 10 scripts (Hindi, Marathi, Gujarati, Bengali, Punjabi,
Odia, Assamese, Sanskrit, Tamil, Telugu, Kannada, Malayalam, Urdu and English) with the
amount of text of each language proportional to the size of its Sangraha split, so that the
largest language has about 40 times the text of the smallest, at a 64k vocabulary
